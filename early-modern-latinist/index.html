The premise

A historical formation, not a learned persona

Latin was the infrastructure of early modern learning. It connected grammar schools to universities, classical literature to law and medicine, and ancient authorities to contemporary argument.

The agent therefore follows a cumulative curriculum: reading the classical canon, crossing late antique and medieval Latin, reconstructing humanist exercises, studying university and disciplinary registers, and only then transferring that competence to Hume.

The editorial method

One passage, four investigations

Translation is only one layer. Select each view to see what a research dossier must establish.

First: preserve the evidence

What exactly is being read?

Identify the work, manifestation, surviving copy, scanned page, transcription, and extracted passage. Record typography, punctuation, note attachment, and every normalization.

  • Work ≠ edition
  • Edition ≠ copy
  • Page image ≠ OCR

Then: parse before polishing

What does this Latin permit?

Establish morphology and syntax, retain consequential ambiguity, and classify chronology, region, genre, discipline, and register without “correcting” later Latin into classical norms.

  • Diplomatic text
  • Controlling syntax
  • Literal and contextual translation

Next: reconstruct transmission

Where did the wording come from?

Separate the ultimate ancient source from the immediate edition, commentary, dictionary, commonplace book, historian, or other intermediary plausibly used by the early modern writer.

  • Compare candidate editions
  • Test distinctive variants
  • Keep alternatives alive

Finally: return to the argument

What work does the Latin perform?

Ask whether it supplies evidence, invokes authority, preserves technical precision, marks readership, stages irony, characterizes a speaker, or links historiographical traditions.

  • Evidence
  • Inference
  • Alternatives and uncertainty

Primary case study

Latin in Hume’s
History of England

For each passage, the analysis must establish its meaning, textual identity, route of transmission, and function within Hume’s historical argument.

The Hume programme begins with a passage-level inventory across volumes and editions. It records the printed Latin and its typography, surrounding English, source type, persistence and revision, and whether the passage is Hume’s composition, quotation, paraphrase, translation, or later editorial matter.

What these results do not establish

They do not validate general Latin mastery, independent source attribution, Scottish curricular reconstruction, disciplinary Latin, or readiness to edit Hume. The tests used bounded source universes, were self-scored, and still require independent Latinist review.
